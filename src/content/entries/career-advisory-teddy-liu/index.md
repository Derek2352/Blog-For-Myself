## What it was

A career advisory conversation with **Mr. Teddy Liu**, General Manager of Group Audit
and Management Services at **New World Development** — which means he runs internal
audit for one of Hong Kong's largest property groups, and is accountable for its
corporate governance, risk management and compliance.

The half of his role I find more interesting is the other half. Alongside audit he
drives the group's innovation process, with culture as the starting point rather than
the afterthought, and owns corporate strategy, process improvement and operational
reviews. Those two jobs usually belong to people who disagree with each other. Audit
asks whether a claim survives inspection; innovation proposes claims nobody has
inspected yet. He does both.

Twenty-nine years inside the same group, across accounting and finance, company
secretarial work, M&A, corporate reorganisation, change management, audit and risk,
and talent development — including building the group's corporate university and the
curricula that run inside it. He's a CPA, served a two-year term on the Council of the
Hong Kong Institute of Certified Public Accountants from 2021, and holds a Juris Doctor
from CUHK and an MBA from HKU.

## What I did

I brought the question I bring to most of these conversations — what should I be doing
now — and got an answer about *record-keeping*, which was not what I expected.

Two things. **Log the experience as it happens, on LinkedIn, with the reflection
attached** — not the role and the dates, but what the thing actually taught me. And
then the larger one: **build a personal site**, somewhere the trips and the projects
can exist at full detail, and somewhere the personality is visible rather than
compressed out of the way.

That second suggestion is why this page exists. You are reading the deliverable. Every
entry here — the Cantonese microcopy on a split-bill screen, the four days of commits
behind a research tool, the ghost in the hallway in a sixty-second film — exists in
this form because of a conversation in which someone said the detail was worth keeping
and the reflection was the part with value.

## What I learned

The advice landed the way it did because of who gave it. From someone whose profession
is testing whether an organisation's claims survive inspection, "keep a record" isn't
administrative advice. It's evidentiary. A CV is a list of assertions; the reflection
underneath is what makes them supported. An auditor knows the difference between a
statement and a statement you can stand behind, and so should anyone writing about
their own work.

That turned out to be a habit I'd half-formed without naming it. The competition entry
I'm proudest of makes every persona trait cite the post it came from; the research tool
underneath it refuses to publish a claim that can't name its source. Being told to hold
my own account of myself to the same standard was less a new idea than being shown
where an existing one also applied.

The other half — *let the personality show* — is the part I'd have been most likely to
dismiss. It sounds like decoration. It isn't. Everyone applying alongside me has a
comparable transcript and a similar list of societies, and the transcript is precisely
the part that distinguishes nobody. What distinguishes is what you noticed, what you
found funny, what you got wrong and how you talk about it. That isn't softer than the
credentials; it's the only genuinely scarce thing in the pile.

And a smaller one, from the shape of his own career: twenty-nine years in one group,
and the range inside it is enormous — finance, legal, M&A, audit, teaching. Depth and
variety turn out not to be opposites. That's a more encouraging model than the one I'd
been carrying around, where staying somewhere long enough to be trusted and doing
several different things looked like a choice.

## How it felt

Slightly recursive, and I find it funny. He suggested a blog; I built the blog; the
blog now contains an entry about being told to build it. Writing that paragraph felt
like standing between two mirrors.

Underneath the joke, something I didn't expect. "Build a personal site" sounds like a
weekend. It became months — a content model, an accessibility pass, a cat that walks
along the bottom of the screen, and a long run of corrections where I found out how
much of my own first draft was decorative rather than true. The suggestion was small
and the work wasn't, and I'm glad nobody warned me.

The part I'd want him to know is that the reflection turned out to be the difficult
bit, exactly as he implied. Listing what I did takes an afternoon. Working out what I
made of it, honestly enough that it survives being read back later, is the part that's
still going.
