## What it was

A seven-day youth cultural exchange in **Wuyishan, Fujian**, and I am going as
**組長** — group leader. The trip produces a retrospective short film, and the part
I took on is the two-minute animated section at its centre.

Wuyishan sits in Nanping, in the north of Fujian, and is one of only four places in
China listed as **both** a cultural and a natural World Heritage site. The
programme's booklet runs on five threads — Zhu Xi's neo-Confucianism, tea, the
landscape, the old towns, and art. In practice that means a bamboo raft down the
**Nine-Bend River**, fifteen *li* of it, past the two peaks the local legend is
named for; the climb up **Tianyou Peak**, 408.8m and "the finest sight in Wuyi",
which is where you can see all thirty-six peaks at once; a tea plantation, where
you pick to the *one bud, two leaves* standard and then follow the leaf through the
shaking sieve, the pan and the rolling; **Zhu Xi's academy**, founded in 1183, where
he taught for about a decade and wrote the commentary that became the standard answer
in the imperial examinations for centuries afterwards; **Wufu town**, where he
arrived at fourteen and stayed for most of fifty years; and the national park's
museums, down to a hall holding several hundred butterfly species.

And one evening at **《印象大紅袍》** — *Impression Dahongpao* — which is the stop
this entry is really about, because it is the one I did homework for. It is an
outdoor performance staged nightly at the foot of Dawang Peak, the fifth in Zhang
Yimou, Wang Chaoge and Fan Yue's *Impression* series; it opened on **29 March 2010**
and by May 2025 had run about **6,900 times** for roughly **ten million people**.

The thing that makes it worth studying is structural rather than decorative. The
auditorium is a 1,988-seat steel disc that **rotates through 360°**, about five
minutes per turn and roughly thirteen turns across the seventy minutes, ringed by
four performance areas — teahouse, terraced fields, flat ground, water — with fifteen
screens set into the real landscape. **The scenery never changes. The audience does.**
The official description of the venue is "the world's largest teahouse", which sounds
like marketing until you are sitting in it being handed a cup of rock tea.

## What I did

I wrote the research and the brief the animation gets built from, before the trip
rather than after it, so that the group arrives knowing what it is looking for. A
show you have already taken apart is a show you can film; a show you see cold is
seventy minutes of being impressed, and then nothing to cut.

**The show, taken apart.** A scene-by-scene account of all seventy minutes, then
eleven segments broken down shot by shot — the Tang banquet on the three-tier
stage, the bamboo-grove duel, the raft on the water, the tea-terrace shaking-and-
sieving sequence, the scholar's journey and the legend of the red robe, the
eighteen steps of Wuyi tea ceremony, the harvest, and the finale with its abacus
dance and its wedding. Each one ends in a motif list written for a storyboard
rather than for a reader: the things that are *drawable*, separated from the things
that are merely true.

**An art direction, and the one place it conflicts.** The animation is ink-wash —
*shuimo* — crossed with the high-contrast new-guofeng wuxia register: solid black
silhouettes on rice-paper ground, *feibai* dry-brush streaks for speed, splashed
ink for transitions, and vermilion as the only colour. That choice is not imported.
The show is already using four of ink-wash's core devices on stage — mist,
silhouette, calligraphy, and empty space.

The conflict is colour, and it is a real one. **A large part of the show's
narration runs on hue**: the clearest case is the tea-processing sequence, where the
lighting pushes from new-shoot green through sun-dried gold to oxidised red, and
that spectrum *is* the process diagram. Ink-wash is black, white and cinnabar. The
resolution is not to delete the colour story but to **transpose it** — hue-shift
becomes ink-density shift across the five traditional grades, and the vermilion is
rationed to the few places that genuinely have to be red: the liquor in the cup, the
red rim on a green leaf, the lanterns, and the tea carried out into the audience.
Red everywhere and the style collapses.

**A storyboard that a generator can actually execute.** Four thirty-second shots
cut to two minutes exactly, each split into six five-second beats with an English
prompt attached — five seconds because that is what current video models hold
together, so a "thirty-second prompt" is really a scene description that still has
to be generated in pieces and joined. The four shots map onto the show's four
performance areas, so watching the film is one rotation of the auditorium. The
tension curve runs high–mid–highest–resolve while the red runs a point–a line–none–
full frame: **the two are deliberately out of phase, so the most violent passage is
the plainest and the quietest ending is the reddest.**

**And the boundary, written down before anyone starts.** Redrawing a staged image
in a different medium is quotation; lifting the material is copying. So the brief
rules things out explicitly: no line of the original script, no bar of the original
score, no footage or stills in the finished piece — those stay inside the report as
reference. It credits the source in plain words on the end card rather than implying
any relationship, and it flags the two places to keep away from generative tools
entirely: **the calligraphy and the seal**, because every current video model breaks
Chinese characters, and those are the two frames that most need a hand.
