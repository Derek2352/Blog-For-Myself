## What it was

A career advisory session with **Mr. Daniel Yen**, **Head of HR at BOCI-Prudential
Asset Management**. The subject was interview skills.

What makes that worth an entry isn't the topic, which every careers office covers. It's
the seat he occupies. Most interview advice comes from people who are describing the
process from outside it — coaches, guides, students one year ahead. He runs hiring. It's
the difference between a spectator explaining a rule and a referee explaining it, and
the two explanations are not the same explanation.

I should be straight about what follows. I remember what the session was about and I
remember one specific piece of advice. The rest — the reasoning below, the way I've
framed it — is mine, worked out afterwards. None of it is a quotation and none of it
should be read as his words.

## What I took from it

**On interviews, the thing I hadn't understood is the asymmetry.** I had been preparing
for interviews as a performance: rehearse the answers, deliver them well, don't
fumble. But the person across the table isn't watching a performance. They're doing a
job — filling a role, managing the risk of choosing wrong, and comparing me against a
queue of people who all arrived that week. Once you see it that way the question stops
being *how do I sound good* and becomes *what does this person need in order to justify
choosing me*. Those produce quite different answers.

The second thing follows from it, and it's the part I think only someone in his seat
would know to say. He has heard the answer to "tell me about a time you worked in a
team" several hundred times. Which means a *correct* answer is worth almost nothing —
correct is the floor, and everyone clears it. What survives the afternoon is the answer
that was specific enough to remember: a real number, a real disagreement, a thing that
actually went wrong. I had been optimising for being right. The scarce quality is being
memorable, and the raw material for that is detail you can only get by having actually
paid attention to something.

**And then the advice I do remember, close to verbatim in substance: while you're
interning, watch how the bank operates. Don't just get the duties done.**

I've been turning that over, because it's a bigger instruction than it first sounds. An
internship hands you two separate things. There's a task list, which is what you're
assessed on and what fills the time. And there's a vantage point — a few weeks of being
allowed inside an institution, with a pass, permitted to ask questions that would sound
naive from anyone else. The task list is finite and someone else defines it. The vantage
point is the part that expires, and nobody hands you an agenda for it.

Sitting where I am, in the Loans Division of a Bank-Wide Operation Department, the
questions that only make sense from inside are the ones about *why the machine has the
shape it has*. Why does this control exist — what went wrong once that put it there?
What actually happens when a document is incomplete: who does it go back to, and how
long does that cost? Who signs off, and what are they personally carrying if they're
wrong? Where are the handoffs between teams, since that's where things get lost? What's
still done by hand, and is that inertia or is there a reason? None of that is on my task
list. All of it is visible from my desk.

## What I learned

The two halves of the session turn out to be one instruction pointing in the same
direction, which I don't think I noticed at the time.

Interviews are won with specific, observed detail. An internship is one of the very few
places you can go and *acquire* specific, observed detail. So "watch how the bank
operates" isn't a separate piece of advice about being a good intern — it's the supply
line for every interview I'll sit in the next five years. Doing the tasks well is what
makes me a good intern this summer. Watching the machine is what makes me a candidate
with something to say about banking in three years' time, when everyone else in the room
also has an internship on their CV and describes it in the same six words.

The other thing I've taken from it is smaller and more practical: curiosity has a
deadline. I have access right now that I won't have in September, and I'll have it again
only if I'm employed there. Questions are cheap while you're the intern and expensive
afterwards.

## How it felt

Slightly exposing, in a way I found useful. You can't perform at someone who interviews
people for a living — they've seen the technique, and any polish you apply reads as
polish. The only thing left is to be specific and to actually know things, which is
harder and also more of a relief.

The advice arrived at exactly the right moment, which I appreciate more now than I did
then. It isn't a lesson I filed away; it's an instruction I'm carrying out. I'm about
four weeks into the internship as I write this, and "watch how it actually works" is a
thing I do on purpose at a specific desk, in a specific department, most days.

And one honest admission, since this site is supposed to be honest. I kept the substance
of what he told me and lost the wording, and I've had to rebuild the reasoning myself to
write this. It's a small argument for writing things down closer to when they happen —
which, as it turns out, is the exact thing another adviser told me a few weeks later.
