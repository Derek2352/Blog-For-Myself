## What it was

The third **One Earth Summit**, organised by the Institute of Sustainability and Technology
and HKUST, under the theme *Collective Flourishing in the Intelligent Age*. It ran across three
threads — AI for Good, Energy and Climate, and Oceans and Nature — with ministers, regulators,
university leaders and founders on one stage. I was there as a **Youth Observer**.

## What I did

I recorded the sessions, photographed the slides that mattered, and afterwards went back through
every session I had a transcript for. The transcripts were
rough, so I checked the figures against the official speeches where they were published. Nine
sessions came out of that, and the notes below are what I kept from each.

## What I learned

### Kevin Choi — AI for a low-carbon future

The Permanent Secretary for Innovation, Technology and Industry framed AI and decarbonisation as
twin revolutions that have to succeed together: green technology to offset AI's growing energy
appetite, and AI to speed up green technology. His three tests — does AI cut emissions, does it
benefit society, does it protect the world we share — became three pillars: capability,
opportunity and inclusion. Hong Kong's compute is at 5,000 PFLOPS today, and the Sandy Ridge
data facility is meant to add 180,000 PFLOPS by 2032, with data centres now facing energy audits
every five years. What stayed with me was the gap he did not dwell on: audits and efficiency
standards make each unit of compute cleaner, but they do not cap the total.

### Panel — investing in the transition at speed and scale

Lisa Sachs of Columbia moderated Andrew Yu Han (Green Climate Fund), Kenny Lam (The Nature
Conservancy) and Pan Liang (BYD). The line I underlined was Sachs's: electric cars are not
winning because people want to save the climate, they are winning because they are better.
BYD's answer to range anxiety was charging, not range — "ready in five, full in nine, cold add
three." The finance lesson was the one closest to my degree: private capital is willing to go
into emerging-market energy, but a project is not financeable until someone de-risks it first,
which is the job of public money and blended finance.

### Arthur Yuen — connecting sustainable finance with climate-resilient growth

The HKMA's Deputy Chief Executive opened with Tuen Mun as a Tang-dynasty gateway on the maritime
Silk Road, and then the numbers: mitigation needs three to four times today's finance flows, and
adaptation in developing countries twelve to fourteen times. His structure was depth before
breadth — common standards, awareness and disclosure first, then connecting capital, people and
technology. The Hong Kong Taxonomy's latest prototype has 39 mitigation activities and 28
adaptation measures, and it is among the first in the region to define adaptation at all, which
matters in a city that had fourteen tropical cyclones and five black rainstorms last year.

### Terry Wong — supercharging battery and energy storage

HKSTP launched its first battery and energy storage report at the summit. Storage is what lets
grids take more solar and wind, and AI's power demand makes it urgent. His slide drew Hong Kong's
part as a loop rather than a line — materials and battery R&D, integration, project finance,
end-of-life recycling, then back to materials. Hong Kong has no gigafactories, so the argument is
about the value around manufacturing rather than manufacturing itself. He also described HKSTP
as an "innovation operating system": hardware, software and the people who use them, which is a
lens I can borrow for any ecosystem I am part of.

### Prof. Ya-Qin Zhang — scaling AI for good

The founding dean of Tsinghua's Institute for AI Industry Research defined AI 3.0 as digital,
physical and biological AI together — bits, atoms and molecules. 2026, for him, is the year
generative AI becomes agentic AI, and agents become the apps and SaaS of the next decade. His
showcase was the Agent Hospital, where AI doctors improve by treating simulated patients. It is
genuinely clever, and the 93% score behind it is a benchmark result inside a simulation, not a
measure of real clinical accuracy — a distinction I want to keep making every time a number like
that is put in front of me.

### Prof. Bin Yang — slow education and selection science

This was the session I came home thinking about. Tsinghua's Bin Yang built it on two questions:
Andrew Yao's — when AI can do more and more, what must the student still do alone? — and Omar
Yaghi's — when we can create almost anything, what is worth choosing? His answer to the first is
*slow education*: knowledge can be given, but ability only grows in the person who walks the hard
road themselves, and when AI does the work, performance improves but no learning happens. In
practice that means two lanes — a safety lane where fundamentals are tested in person without
AI, and an open lane where AI is used to the fullest on ambitious projects. His answer to the
second is Yaghi's *selection science*: science asks why, research asks why not, selection science
asks *why this*. AI answers "can"; somebody still has to answer "worth it".

### Paul Chan — financing innovation for a sustainable future

The Financial Secretary named three linked challenges: paying for the transition, sharing the
benefits of technology, and preparing people for the jobs that follow. The toolkit he described
is the most concrete finance of the day — about US$77 billion of green and sustainable debt
arranged in Hong Kong last year, infrastructure loans securitised to free up capital for new
projects, and catastrophe bonds that pass disaster risk to capital markets. His closing line was
that the intelligent age will be judged not by the power of its machines but by the lives it
improves.

### Huang Runqiu — China's green transformation

The Minister of Ecology and Environment set out China's record — new-energy vehicles leading the
world for eleven years, a national carbon market now covering power, steel, cement and aluminium —
and the next five-year plan's move towards controlling total carbon emissions rather than only
intensity. For a finance student, that shift is the part to watch: absolute caps change what an
emission allowance is worth.

### Leung Chun-ying — climate, health and China's role

He made the case through health rather than carbon: mosquito-borne disease appearing where it
never used to, heat stress among outdoor workers in Hong Kong, and the WHO's estimate of about
250,000 additional deaths a year from climate change between 2030 and 2050. His economic point
was a simple one I keep returning to: the cost of climate change is not in market prices, so
policy has to put it there.

### Putting it together

Read in sequence, the day described one chain: infrastructure, then investable projects, then
trusted standards and markets, with Hong Kong cast — by every government speaker — as the
connector between them. Running underneath it was a quieter thread from the university speakers:
as AI takes over more of the doing, the human part that is left is growth, and judgement about
what is worth doing. Some of the day's claims are also contested, and I want to be able to argue
both sides — whether China's clean-tech scale is a gift of cheap technology to the Global South or
subsidised overcapacity, and whether efficiency rules are enough without absolute limits.
