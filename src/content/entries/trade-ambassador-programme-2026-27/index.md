## What it was

The **Trade Ambassador Programme** (<span lang="zh-Hant">商貿大使計劃</span>) is the Hong Kong
Trade Development Council's year-long programme for tertiary students, running since 2007 to widen
students' understanding of Hong Kong and global trade. Each cohort brings in members from
universities and colleges across the city, and the 2026/27 year runs from October 2026 to June 2027
in five kinds of activity: an orientation briefing, Meet the Economist, guided tours of trade fairs
and conferences, CEO talks, and company visits.

This term is themed **AI, Innovation and Global Trade**: how AI and emerging technologies change the
way businesses operate and open up new markets. Members earn the programme certificate by attending
three of the scheduled activities.

## What I did

I enrolled in September 2026 and joined the 2026/27 cohort as a Trade Ambassador. The term opened
on **9 October** with the Orientation Briefing & Meet the Economist at the HKTDC SME Centre, in the
Convention and Exhibition Centre in Wan Chai, run by HKTDC's SME Relations, Global Network and
Supply Chain team.

The first part was HKTDC itself, in its 60th-anniversary year. It organises its support for
industry into six sector clusters, from finance and professional services to technology and digital
innovation, and three of its services were walked through in turn: **HKTDC Research** for market
intelligence (business analysis, regulatory alerts, market and industry profiles), **Hong Kong Means
Business** for news on its events and on global trade, and **hktdc.com Sourcing**, an online
marketplace matching more than 2 million registered buyers with over 130,000 suppliers, at more
than 20 million connections a year. Then the programme: the five activities and the year they fill.

After that came the economists. HKTDC Research's global, Greater China, and Asian and emerging
markets teams gave a talk called *AI Unleashed: Driving the Future of Global Trade*, which is
written up below. HKTDC's HR team closed with its Internship Programme and Executive Trainee
Programme.

The next invitation was a 25-place guided tour of the **Hong Kong Electronics Fair (Autumn
Edition)** and **electronicAsia** on 14 October, focused on AI and digital entertainment.

### AI and trade, in both directions

The talk's starting point was that AI cuts the cost of trading, in logistics, compliance and
communications. The example on screen was Descartes, which says its AI agent cuts the time spent
researching and analysing global trade data by up to 90%. Other tools on the same slide pull tariff
and classification data into one view, screen shipments for compliance risks, and fill the empty
space in shipping containers.

The WTO's *World Trade Report 2025* puts a number on the whole effect. Across four scenarios, AI
raises global trade by 34–37% by 2040. Digitally deliverable services gain the most, about 40%, and
primary inputs the least, under 10%. The gains are not even, though. Low-income economies get 24% if
the technology gap widens and 32% if they catch up, while high-income economies get about 36% in
every scenario. Catching up matters most to the economies that have least.

The relationship also runs the other way, because trade is what makes AI possible. Every link in the
AI value chain crosses a border: critical minerals, semiconductors, cloud services and the power
behind them, cross-border data flows, and the apps themselves. On the report's reading, future
comparative advantage comes down to access to cheap, sustainable energy, high-quality interoperable
data and compute that scales.

### What it means for Hong Kong

Hong Kong's exports rose **42.5%** year on year in January–August 2026, driven by global demand for
AI-related products and infrastructure. Electronic integrated circuits and microassemblies made up
43% of the total, telephones and network equipment 11%, and computer parts and accessories 10%. Of
Hong Kong's re-exports to the US over the same months, 42.9% were made in Vietnam and 23.8% on the
Chinese Mainland.

On policy, the slides went through the Chief Executive's 2026 Policy Address. Under *Four Centres
and One Hub*, it sets out to deepen AI use in finance, healthcare, legal services, construction,
transport, social welfare, tourism and broadcasting, and to help SMEs upgrade. It names seven areas
for AI risk governance: AI-enabled crime, protecting users who are minors, assessing AI's ethical
risks, safe use across industries, legal liability for accidents caused by AI products, guidelines
for managing AI agents, and AI's impact on employment. It also creates a Commissioner for AI. The
infrastructure behind all this includes the Sandy Ridge data facility cluster, and HKSTP and
SenseTime's June 2026 plan to build Hong Kong's largest domestic AI data centre.

### What it means for jobs

The last part drew on the World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025*. The fastest-growing
roles by net growth are big data specialists, FinTech engineers, and AI and machine learning
specialists. The fastest-declining are postal service clerks, bank tellers, data entry clerks and
cashiers, with accounting, bookkeeping and payroll clerks further down the same list.

People carry out 47% of work tasks today, technology 22%, and the two together 30%. By 2030 the WEF
expects roughly a third each, and the slide put 81.5% of the fall in the human share down to
automation. Of the skills employers expect to need more by 2030, AI and big data comes first,
followed by networks and cybersecurity and technological literacy. Analytical thinking, creative
thinking and resilience remain the core.
