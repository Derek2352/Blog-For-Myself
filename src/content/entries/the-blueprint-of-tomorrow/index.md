## What it was

The Corporate Governance Development Foundation Fund ran a creative video contest with an
unusually hard brief: make corporate governance *watchable*. Two to three minutes, English
voiceover with Chinese and English subtitles, judged by HSUHK academics and by directors from
**The Hong Kong Institute of Directors** — people who do governance for a living and would
notice immediately if I got it wrong. The film was only half of it: there was also a panel,
where you sit in front of those people and defend what you made.

Most governance films fail the same three ways, and naming them was how I found the film.
They're **abstract** — boards, audits, disclosure, concepts with no picture attached. They're
**technical**, in a vocabulary that belongs to lawyers rather than viewers. And they're
**forgettable**, because a checklist is not a story. So the method had to invert: don't
explain governance. Let an audience feel its absence, and then its presence.

Governance sounds abstract until you remember what it's for: keeping boards and management
accountable to the people whose money and trust they hold. So I built the film around a
single decision. A young startup CEO, Alex, is offered a contract she shouldn't sign — and
the timeline splits on her choice. Same company, same scenes, two outcomes — and the mirror
is the argument rather than the device. It shows governance as *causation*, not compliance.

> "Every big idea starts with a choice. Not just what you build… but *how* you build it."

If the film has a thesis, it's the line I ended up defending at the panel: **good governance
is not a checklist — it is a causal architecture.**

## What I did

Two worlds, same company. In the first, the rules get ignored and the collapse arrives as
falling black dominoes — cold light, restless cuts, each failure knocking over the next until
it stops being a business problem and becomes a community one. Then time rewinds, the same
company chooses differently, and a golden seed grows into a tree of light, shot warm and
slow, until the tree becomes the Hong Kong skyline.

Every principle had to land as something you could *see* rather than a caption. A red audit
folder tossed aside; the green one actually read. One founder shouting down the room; three
founders voting. Documents shredded; an email to investors that tells them the truth. A toxic
alleyway; a zero-emission plant. Riders protesting outside the office; a rider paid a bonus.

Underneath that, every scene had to carry two things at once — a governance concept and the
money it moves. That second column is where a finance degree stops being decorative on a film
project and starts doing work. The ignored audit is risk management, and what it
costs is hidden exposure and a higher cost of capital. Shredded files are disclosure, and the
consequence is information asymmetry landing on the share price. The toxic sludge is ESG:
fines, lost green financing, reputational damage. The protesting riders are stakeholder
fairness, and the bill arrives as labour disputes and operational disruption. Shouting at an
intern is accountability, and it compounds quietly into talent attrition. Then the dominoes,
which are systemic risk itself — one breach toppling a structure that looked stable.

The mirrored half is built the same way, only the column reads as value rather than cost. The
green-folder audit is due diligence: decisions made on data, before the fact rather than
after it. The board vote is checks and balances — nobody holding absolute power. Pinning the
code of conduct to the wall is compliance culture, visible rules and a protected
whistleblower. The eco-factory is sustainability, and zero-emission alignment is what unlocks
green finance. Paying the rider properly is fairness. The bow on the video call is real
accountability, a leader owning an outcome in public. And the glowing roots are long-term
value: an ecosystem that compounds instead of collapsing.

My first storyboard ran **eleven shots**. A two-and-a-half minute film needs about **forty-one**,
and finding that out meant rebuilding the whole thing at the right resolution — which turned
out to be where the emotional work actually lived. World A is cut at roughly a second a shot,
handheld, so it feels like losing your footing. World B runs four-second tracking shots so it
feels like breathing again. The pacing does more of the persuading than the script does.

Animation wasn't the fallback; it was the argument. Live-action can't show systemic risk or
ethical culture — you can't point a camera at them — and animation gives an abstraction a
body: falling dominoes, glowing roots. It compresses causation, which matters when you have
two and a half minutes and one cut has to carry a whole chain of consequence. It puts a
boardroom, a factory, a street and a video call in one visual identity with no location or
casting budget. And it lets the exaggeration be symbolic: visibly toxic sludge and a glowing
tree would read as absurd or grotesque if you shot them for real.

The sharper question was why *animated* rather than AI photorealism, and the reasoning there
has aged better than anything else in the project. Synthetic humans sit in the uncanny valley
and trigger
distrust — at precisely the moment the film is asking an audience to think about trust. The
medium would have undercut the message. Beyond that, a governance and ethics submission
cannot put synthetic real people in front of a room of HKIoD directors; the deepfake optics
alone would sink it. Illustration also buys something photorealism can't: the viewer sees
*the CEO*, *the auditor*, *the rider* — roles rather than personalities, which is what lets
the story read as universal instead of as one identifiable company. And a consistent
illustrated style reads as a deliberate artistic choice, where AI photorealism usually reads
as a shortcut.

The craft problems were the interesting part. AI drifts — the same character comes back two
shots later with a different face — so I locked Alex, Ben and Chloe into fixed blueprints and
held them across every generation with Midjourney's `--cref` character reference, alongside
Stable Diffusion for stills. Those keyframes then went through an image-to-video pass across
Runway, Kling and Sora, where the rule I learned the hard way is to describe **only the camera
and the motion**. Mention the face and the model helpfully rebuilds it, worse. I also let
small spoken asides sit under the narration, because a scene where someone quietly swears at
their own decision lands harder than one that only explains.

The shot the whole ending rests on, the tree becoming the city, was the one thing the models
simply couldn't do: they won't morph one object into another cleanly. Asked to try, it handed
me a tree growing out of a skyscraper. Rather than keep arguing with it, I generated both
pushes separately and cross-dissolved them over three seconds in the edit. It reads as
intentional now, which is the only test that matters.

The film ends on a whiteboard. After two and a half minutes of dramatising the thing, a
hand-drawn outro finally names it: **Transparency** — tell the truth, completely and on time.
**Fairness** — treat every stakeholder with equal regard. **Accountability** — own the
outcome, especially the bad ones. **Responsibility** — build for the world the company leaves
behind. Four pillars, named last on purpose, so the audience arrives at the framework having
already felt it.

The theme song is original too — a 125 bpm anthem written for the film and produced with
Suno, fading in on the rewind and carrying the final act. Two of the pillars are in the
chorus, which was not an accident.

> *"With fairness in our hands and hearts to follow, / we'll build a shining city that will
> last! / Yeah, accountability will be our guiding light, / responsibility to make the future
> bright!"*

The deck I took into the panel is the opposite of the film's whiteboard: formal, typographic,
built to be read by directors. It had to be, because the film was aimed at two audiences at
once — HKIoD members and the general public — and had to clear both bars, boardroom
credibility and mass-market clarity, without splitting the difference. It was also designed
to outlive the contest as teaching material for HSUHK and HKIoD.

AI use had to be declared to the judges, which I think is exactly as it should be.

## What I learned

The real discipline was **translation without dilution**: making ESG and disclosure legible
to a general audience without flattening what they actually mean. Every simplification had to
survive contact with someone who knows the subject cold — and that constraint is why the
finance side and the film side stopped feeling like two separate things. The writing had to
be *correct* before it was allowed to be moving.

I also learned to treat a model's limitation as a design brief rather than a defect. The
cross-dissolve isn't a workaround I'm hiding; it's the edit the material asked for once I
stopped insisting the tool do something it can't.

And I learned that rhythm is a tool, not a finishing touch. Going from eleven shots to
forty-one wasn't about filling time — shot length is how you make an audience feel hurried or
safe, and I'd been treating it as an afterthought.

The subtlest thing I had to get right was tone, and I only understood it once I was building
the panel deck. The film opens by showing a company behaving badly — to a room of practising
directors. Play that contemptuously and you've insulted the people you're asking to approve
it. So the failure had to be **cautionary rather than cynical**: instructive, recognisable,
never sneering. Serious enough to screen at an annual dinner, and never pleased with itself.
Working out who is actually in the room, and what they can hear from you, turned out to be
as much of the design as any shot. I also wrote the whole thing against the published judging
criteria rather than guessing at them — which felt less like gaming a contest than reading
the brief properly.

## How it felt

Corporate governance is not a subject I'd have chosen for a film. It reads like homework, and
for a while it behaved like one — the concept was the turn. The moment the parallel worlds
arrived, a boardroom topic became something closer to a thriller, and I actually wanted to
make it.

The middle stretch was mostly stubbornness. Shot 35 took the longest and shows the least: a
model insisting that a tree growing out of a skyscraper was what I'd asked for, over and over,
until I stopped trying to win and went around it. Finding out you can simply *edit* past a
limitation felt less like a compromise than a promotion.

Winning Champion — and being recognised by the people who practise governance
professionally — was the moment my two worlds stopped feeling like a split and started
feeling like an advantage.

The deck ends on the line the whole film is really about, and I still think it's right about
more than governance: bad architecture collapses, and good architecture compounds.
